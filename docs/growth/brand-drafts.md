### Post 1: the six-second myth

**Long (LinkedIn)**

> Six seconds is the kind of number that sticks when nobody writes back.
>
> It comes from a job site's 2012 report on its own product. The site watched 30 recruiters.
>
> The strongest study since looked at 2,043 resume reviews by recruiters in one US state. It found no fixed timer. What it did find: recruiters who spent longer on the Experience section were more likely to move a resume forward.
>
> That's a correlation, not a cause. They may have lingered because the resume was relevant, or because it was hard to follow. Either way, it's a better target than a stopwatch. Put your recent, relevant roles where they're easy to find, and say what you did in them.
>
> Both studies, with what each one can and can't tell you: [link]

**Short (Threads, X; 256 characters, 280 with the link)**

> Six seconds is the kind of number that sticks when nobody writes back. It comes from a job site's 2012 report on 30 recruiters. A 2023 study of 2,043 resume reviews found no timer. Longer looks at Experience went with a yes. Make that section easy to find. [link]

### Post 2: one typo

**Long (LinkedIn)**

> No study we've found tests whether one typo costs you the interview. Five is another story.
>
> In a 2020 experiment, 445 recruiters in Belgium rated graduate resumes with zero, two, or five spelling errors. The experience on the page was identical. The five-error versions scored 1.85 points lower on a 0 to 10 interview scale, and the recruiters rated those applicants lower on conscientiousness, people skills, and mental ability.
>
> What it doesn't show: what a single typo does, whether the result holds outside Dutch-language resumes in Belgium, or what happens to real callbacks. These were ratings.
>
> So yes, run the spell-check. Do it last, after the fixes that change what a recruiter learns about you.
>
> [link]

### Post 3: referrals

**Long (LinkedIn)**

> Same resume, different door.
>
> One recruiting software company looked at more than 38 million applications in its customers' data. 40% of referred applications reached an interview. Of applications sent in cold, 3% did.
>
> That's a vendor's data on its own customers, so hold the exact numbers loosely. The direction isn't new: at one US financial services firm the New York Fed studied, referrals were 6% of applications and 29% of hires.
>
> What a referral changes is who opens your resume. It doesn't change the read once it's open. Same page, same questions.
>
> So ask the people you know. Then make sure the page holds up when someone finally reads it. [link]

## Community answer templates

Each is useful with no link at all. Rewrite the first line around the person's actual post, and use the optional link only where the community's rules allow, saying it's ours. If they posted their resume, add one specific observation about a real line in it; that's worth more than the whole template.

### 1. "Hundreds of applications, no callbacks. What's wrong with my resume?"

> [Name their situation in their words: "Two years in product ops and 80 applications with nothing back."] That's rough, and the silence is the worst part because it tells you nothing. Here's how we'd narrow it down.
>
> First, where is it stopping? No screens at all across a lot of applications usually points at the resume's first read or at which roles you're applying to. Screens that go nowhere point at the interview, which is a different fix.
>
> Then three checks on the resume:
>
> 1. Cover everything below the top third. From what's left, could a stranger say what you do and at what level? If not, start there.
> 2. For your last two roles, does each line say what changed and what your part was? "Managed vendor relationships" says what the job was. Something like "Cut vendor onboarding from six weeks to three by rewriting the intake checklist" says what you did. (That's an example; use your own facts and numbers, and only ones you can stand behind.)
> 3. Do your titles match the jobs you're applying for? If your title was internal jargon, add the plain version next to it, like "Data Analyst (Associate II)", so nobody has to translate.
>
> And the part a resume can't fix: in one recruiting software company's data on more than 38 million applications, 40% of referred applications reached an interview, against 3% sent in cold. If you know anyone at a company on your list, ask. It changes who opens the resume.

### 2. "Is the ATS rejecting me? Do I need a plain template?"

> Probably not the way it's usually described. Two widely used applicant tracking systems, Greenhouse and Lever, document what their parsers do: pull your text into fields like name, contact details, and work history. If a file won't parse, it stays attached and someone enters the details by hand. Where their documentation describes automatic rejection, it runs on your answers to the application's questions, not on fonts or columns.
>
> What we'd do with that:
>
> - Answer the application questions carefully and truthfully. That's where an automatic no can actually happen.
> - Check the parse yourself in a minute: open your PDF, select all, and paste it into a plain text editor. If your name, titles, and dates come out in a sensible order, a parser will most likely manage too. If the columns come out scrambled, keep a one-column version for applications.
> - Then stop writing for the robot. Every employer configures its system differently, so nobody outside can promise how yours is set up. But once your resume is in, a person reads it, and that's the read worth writing for.

Optional link: `/research/ats-myths?utm_source=reddit&utm_medium=community&utm_campaign=r-<subreddit>&utm_content=answer`

### 3. "How do I explain the gap on my resume?"

> You can stop drafting the apology. The largest test we know of, a preregistered UK experiment with 9,022 employers, found that a line explaining a caregiving gap did no better than leaving the gap unexplained. The version that did best listed how many years the person spent in each job.
>
> What we'd do:
>
> - Don't hide dates altogether. It tends to raise the very question you're trying to answer, and applications and background checks ask for them anyway.
> - If you did something relevant during the gap (contract work, a project, a course you finished), list it like any other entry. If not, leave the space alone.
> - Keep one plain sentence ready for when someone asks, and say it without apologizing. For example: "I took two years to care for a family member, and I'm back full time." Use your real reason, in as few words as you're comfortable with.
> - Put the effort into the roles before and after. That's what gets read.
>
> One caveat: that study was the UK, caregiving gaps specifically, and it changed the cover letter too. A gap you're in right now also reads differently from one that later work has closed. In an older Swedish experiment, a year out between two past jobs made no measurable difference to callbacks, while current time out of work mattered more.

Optional link: `/research/gaps-and-pivots?utm_source=reddit&utm_medium=community&utm_campaign=r-<subreddit>&utm_content=answer`
