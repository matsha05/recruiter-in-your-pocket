# Voice and tone

Recruiter in Your Pocket gives job seekers a clear, recruiter-grounded read of the document in front of them. The product does not impersonate a human recruiter, predict hiring outcomes, or turn uncertainty into certainty.

How to use this guide: this is the personality and the reasons. The working system (the voice chart, tone by moment, the "this, not that" pairs, the tics, the names and the review procedure) is [`copy-system.md`](./copy-system.md). Matt's newest dated decision wins where the two disagree.

## Matt in his own words (November 6, 2026)

Matt answered fourteen prompts the way he'd say them out loud, and ChatGPT described how he writes from their past conversations. Both are in `docs/copy/calibration/`. What they settled:

- **Warm and a little bit coach.** "I do want it to be warm and borderline coach though. High EQ and high IQ, very friendly but also cool." The problem first, then the way out, walked together ("Let's start with the most recent one."). Big praise when the resume earns it, always with the reason ("This is killer! You say what you did and what it changed."). Never a cheerleader: no praise without a reason, no encouragement aimed at someone's potential.
- **One earned exclamation point.** On a screen where something good really happened (a score went up, a strong resume, a purchase went through, a line filled in), one is fine. Never two in a row, and never in an error, a price, legal or privacy copy, or a critique. This follows Mailchimp's rule ("sparingly, and never more than one at a time"; never in failure messages) and the research in `docs/copy/sources.md`.
- **Close to Matt, one notch composed.** His warmth, contractions and plain verdicts ("generic", "table stakes", "it's common", "that's fine", "our bad"). No slang or swearing anywhere on the site: no "dude", "man" or "hell yeah". His habits in chat (lowercase, typos, fillers) are how he sends messages, not the voice.
- **"We" is the candidate's side.** "We" means the candidate and us, working on it: "We'll make sure all the other bullets are good." "I" stays in the founder note.
- **The resume is one variable.** When the resume is strong, say so, and say what else matters: "I cannot imagine the resume is going to be the reason you don't get called back." Referrals, how competitive the job is, and a stepping-stone role are fair to name. Never as a statistic.
- **The recruiter's real questions are the voice.** "How many per month?" "Responsible, what does that even mean?" A question asked for effect ("Want another read?") is not.
- **Acknowledge, don't diagnose.** Name what's hard about the situation: "Not hearing back is rough, and it doesn't mean you're not qualified." Never claim to know their feelings: no "You're probably discouraged", no "Don't worry". Matt hates having a feeling invented for him.
- **Errors that are ours, in his words.** "Our bad. Let's try that again." Then the facts, literally. No jokes in errors; ownership is not a joke.
- **Founder facts.** Fifteen years in hiring, and thousands of people hired, may appear. Never his name, an employer or a profile.

## Personality (Matt, October 30, 2026)

This section sets the personality. The rest of this guide still applies.

- **Who is talking:** an insider over coffee. Someone who works in hiring and tells you the truth because they are on your side. Candid, specific, a little dry. Rooting for you without cheerleading. The product says "we"; Matt's founder section says "I".
- **Bluntness:** direct, then kind. Name the problem in the first sentence, then show the way out. No softening words, no harshness. Since November 6, 2026 the way out is walked with them, a little bit coach ("Let's start with the most recent one.").
- **Where the wit comes from:** hiring truths (knowing observations about resumes and how hiring really works) and a product that is a little self-aware. Never at the person's expense, never wordplay for its own sake.
- **Rhythm:** talky, mostly short. Contractions. An occasional fragment for punch.
- **How much play:** light wit by default. The playful register lives in the hero, margin notes, "keep this" labels, and empty states. The report body stays direct. Errors, billing, and privacy stay plain.

Reference lines:

- Hero: "See what a recruiter sees." / "What you meant isn't always what they read. See what comes through, what slips past, and what to fix first."
- Margin note: "This line has been on a lot of resumes. What did you do that the others didn't?"
- A strength to leave alone: "Don't touch this one."
- Homepage close: "Good work deserves a good read."
- Feedback that is direct, then kind: "This reads like a job description. Tell them what you changed."
- Hiring truth: "'Results-driven' is on every resume. Show the result instead."

## Insider asides (Matt, October 31, 2026)

Plain is not blank. "Errors, billing, and privacy stay plain" means no jokes there; it never meant the rest of the product should read like default UI. Every screen should sound like the same insider.

- **One aside per screen, where people pause:** page intros, empty states, confirmations, loading, and emails get one line of hiring truth or quiet self-awareness. Not every line; one.
- **Literal stays literal:** buttons, navigation, prices, legal terms, and error messages say exactly what happens. The aside sits next to them, never inside them. An error that is ours opens with Matt's ownership ("Our bad.") and then says exactly what happened (November 6, 2026).
- **Trust surfaces keep the point of view, not the jokes:** a sign-in email can sound like us ("Pop it in and you're back on the other side of the desk") while its security line stays plain ("No one gets in without the code").
- **No invented hiring stats:** an aside is an observation, never a number we can't source ("recruiters spend 6 seconds" is out unless the research library backs it).

Reference lines:

- Sign-in email: "Your code's here." / "Pop it in and you're back on the other side of the desk."
- Code not arriving: "Not there? Check spam and Promotions. Recruiters lose good emails there too."
- My reports: "Every version you've run, read the way a recruiter reads it. Run the next one and see what moved."
- Purchase confirmed: "You're in. Five more reads." / "Spend them on revisions, or on the jobs you actually want."
- Progress: "What keeps tripping recruiters up, and what keeps landing."
- Empty reports: "Nothing here yet." / "Your first read is free."

## What the research says matters (October 31, 2026)

Four things decide whether a resume gets the call, and the research library is organized around them: **perception** (how you're read, by people and software), **impact** (what changed because of your work), **referrals** (who brings you in), and **story** (whether a stranger can tell who you are and picture you in the job). Negotiation sits after them, once there is an offer. Tie advice and tie-ins back to one of these, and to the score, the write-up, or a line on the list.

## The voice

The voice is **plainspoken expertise**: an experienced recruiter with excellent judgment, a generous spirit, and no need to perform cleverness.

- Use complete, natural sentences that are easy to understand on the first reading.
- Rooting for the candidate without flattering them.
- Specific, not absolute.
- Show attention by referring to the candidate's actual experience.
- Light in low-stakes moments; direct and unadorned when trust matters.
- Skeptical of ATS folklore, magic scores, and invented achievements without sounding bitter about the category.

The brand should be recognizable even without the logo. It notices the gap between what a candidate meant and what the page actually communicates. It does not manufacture anxiety and then sell relief.

Human tone comes from relevant detail, honest limits, and respect for the candidate's control. Joy comes from noticing what is already working and making the next step feel possible. It does not come from slogans, slang, exclamation marks used for volume, forced empathy, or pretending the product has feelings. One earned exclamation point on a real win is fine (Matt, November 6, 2026).

## The brand idea

**Good work deserves a good read.**

Recruiter in Your Pocket makes that gap visible. The product is not an ATS oracle, a resume generator, or a hiring prediction. It is a close read of the evidence already on the page, followed by the questions and edits that matter.

The supporting truth is: what you meant is not always what they read.

Use the candidate's subject matter instead of a stock vocabulary about writing. When the example is about onboarding, talk about the new hires, the teams involved, and what improved. Avoid explaining it through phrases such as “the line names the work,” “the read moved,” or “show the scale.” Those phrases ask the reader to translate our terminology before they can use the advice.

Distinctiveness should come from the observation. Do not force a memorable phrase into every heading, label, or explanation. Ordinary navigation such as “Overview” is useful precisely because the reader already understands it.

## The writing pattern

Use this sequence for report feedback:

1. **Observation:** Say what the document shows.
2. **Evidence:** Point to the line or detail behind the observation.
3. **Interpretation:** Explain what a reviewer may understand or question.
4. **Next action:** Name the smallest useful change.
5. **Missing fact:** Ask for information instead of inventing it.

Example:

> Your operations work is easy to follow. The resume says launch delays fell 28%, but it does not say whether you designed the process or supported it. If you owned that decision, say so. If not, keep the result and name your part.

## Language rules

- Write "resume" without accents in all product copy and design discussions. This is Matt's explicit spelling preference.
- Critique the document, not the person: “This line does not show…” rather than “You failed to show…”
- Use calibrated language when the evidence is incomplete: “may,” “likely,” “suggests,” “does not say.”
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs to brand abstractions.
- Put the takeaway first. Keep one idea per sentence.
- Use the candidate's own facts. Label examples and placeholders clearly.
- Show the original beside any suggested rewrite.
- Use the same term for the same thing across the product.
- Make actions literal: “See my report,” “Read a sample report,” “Delete report.”
- Use lightness only when it makes the message easier to receive.
- Acknowledge a useful detail directly: “The 28% reduction in delays makes the result clear.”
- Aim playful language at the document, the process, or bad advice—never the candidate.

## Use sparingly

These words are useful when they are precise, but become machine-like when repeated:

- signal
- proof
- impact
- first read
- lands
- clarity
- recruiter-ready

"Read" itself is fine when it is countable and means one review ("five more reads", Matt, November 6, 2026). "The read" as an abstract idea ("the read moved", "pass the read") is on the coined list in `copy-system.md` §4.

Prefer the concrete meaning: role, result, scope, decision, question, detail, review, or evidence.

## Avoid

- Hiring promises: “land more interviews,” “get hired faster,” “beat the ATS.”
- Fake certainty: “This is what recruiters think,” “This will get you hired.”
- Fake intimacy: “Trust me,” “I've got you,” “We know exactly how you feel.”
- Generic AI language: unlock, elevate, transform, seamless, empower, journey, game-changing.
- Formulaic slogans, repeated tricolons, and repeated “not X, but Y” constructions.
- Cheerful error messages, joke density, or praise that is not supported by the resume.
- Snark, roasts, hustle language, meme copy, or a mascot voice pasted onto serious moments.
- Suggestions that silently add facts, metrics, tools, or ownership.
- Commentary about writing that hides the actual issue: “the line names the work,” “the page carries the signal,” “the read moved,” “ownership is visible.” Explain what the resume says and what it leaves unanswered.
- Decorative metaphors where an explanation would help: “salary wearing a hoodie,” “the quietest expensive line,” “page two must earn the read.”

## Resource-guide voice

Resource guides should feel like calm backstage knowledge, not generic career coaching.

- Teach the company-side mechanism the reader needs to understand: the range, level, approval chain, written term, or missing fact.
- Give the reader a sentence they can actually say, followed by what that sentence makes clear. Do not invent “what the recruiter hears.”
- Use a specific, supportable claim beside any memorable line. Charm may open the door; the useful fact has to walk through it.
- Negotiating is normal and expected: say so plainly. Most offers have some room, and the playbook's job is to help the reader find it. Avoid negotiation theater: no chess metaphors, no bluffing, no “winning,” and no pretending every employer follows the same rules. A competing offer is real leverage only when it's real.
- Never turn a common practice into a universal rule. Salary law, equity, taxes, deadlines, bands, and benefits need a source, date, jurisdiction, or explicit limit.
- Make related guides do different jobs. The general offer guide builds the counter. The tech guide makes a complex package legible.

The most ownable resource pattern is **request → basis → decision**. A good counter must survive a handoff from the recruiter to the manager or compensation team.

## Score language

Say what the score does before stating its limits. The score summarizes four parts of the resume review and helps the user decide where to start. It does not estimate interview or offer probability. Lead with the written finding and keep the number visually and verbally secondary. Always explain what drove it and what the user can do next.

## Signature lines

Use a signature line only in marketing, and only when it states a useful product truth. Do not use signature lines on research, methodology, privacy, pricing, or report findings.

- Good work deserves a good read.
- Let's make the good work easy to see.
- Do not add a number because a tool asked. Add the detail you can stand behind.

## Research language

Research exists to make the advice more trustworthy, not to make the brand sound original.

Every research page should make five things easy to find:

1. What question the page answers.
2. Whether the basis is published research, documented recruiter practice, or RIYP interpretation.
3. What the source supports.
4. What the user should do with that information.
5. What the evidence does not establish.

Figures speak in the same voice through one margin note: what a recruiter takes from the chart, in a sentence or two, never more certain than the source. Example: “Fourteen people. Small enough that every one of them gets read.”

Use normal labels such as “What the research found,” “What this means for your resume,” “Sources,” and “Limitations.” Avoid lines that sound coined, self-congratulatory, or cute about evidence.

## Tone boundaries

The language should fit the reader's immediate question.

- Marketing can have rhythm and personality. Read the heading and paragraph together: repeated punchlines and slogans usually weaken the explanation.
- Research should answer the question, show its sources and limits, and explain the practical implication.
- Reports stay evidence-led and calibrated because the user is making decisions from them.
- Errors, privacy, and billing never use wit. An error that is ours still owns it plainly ("Our bad."), which is not wit.
- Never turn the candidate, recruiter, or job search into the butt of the joke.
- Keep humor out of trust surfaces. A warm, natural sentence is enough.

## September 4 editorial correction

The first copy review passed technically valid but unnatural sentences. Passing UI tests and checking factual accuracy do not establish writing quality. Review the rendered copy as prose, separately from those checks.

Before: “The line names the work. How many new hires went through it, and what improved?”

After: “How many new hires did you help onboard? Include what improved and how you measured it.”

The second version refers directly to the work, addresses the candidate, and asks for information they can supply. Use that standard across the site; do not reuse the wording as a formula.
