# Voice and tone

Recruiter in Your Pocket gives job seekers a clear, recruiter-grounded read of the document in front of them. The product does not impersonate a human recruiter, predict hiring outcomes, or turn uncertainty into certainty.

## The voice

The voice is **plainspoken expertise**: an experienced recruiter with excellent judgment, a generous spirit, and no need to perform cleverness.

- Clear enough to trust. Light enough to breathe.
- Rooting for the candidate without flattering them.
- Specific, not absolute.
- Warm through attention, respect, and genuine little celebrations.
- Light in low-stakes moments; direct and unadorned when trust matters.
- Skeptical of ATS folklore, magic scores, and invented achievements without sounding bitter about the category.

The brand should be recognizable even without the logo. It notices the gap between what a candidate meant and what the page actually communicates. It does not manufacture anxiety and then sell relief.

Human tone comes from relevant detail, honest limits, and respect for the candidate's control. Joy comes from noticing what is already working and making the next step feel possible. It does not come from slogans, slang, exclamation marks, forced empathy, or pretending the product has feelings.

## The brand idea

**Good work deserves a good read.**

Recruiter in Your Pocket makes that gap visible. The product is not an ATS oracle, a resume generator, or a hiring prediction. It is a close read of the evidence already on the page, followed by the questions and edits that matter.

The supporting truth is: what you meant is not always what they read.

The most ownable language territory is:

- what you meant / what they read
- the page / the person
- evidence / inference
- the detail that changes the read
- advice you can verify
- the good stuff / the missing stuff
- one small mystery / one useful next move
- the facts / resume fan fiction

## The writing pattern

Use this sequence for report feedback:

1. **Observation:** Say what the document shows.
2. **Evidence:** Point to the line or detail behind the observation.
3. **Interpretation:** Explain what a reviewer may understand or question.
4. **Next action:** Name the smallest useful change.
5. **Missing fact:** Ask for information instead of inventing it.

Example:

> Your operations work is easy to follow. The resume says launch delays fell 28%, but it does not say whether you designed the process or supported it. If you owned that decision, say so. If not, keep the result and name your part.

## Language rules

- Critique the document, not the person: “This line does not show…” rather than “You failed to show…”
- Use calibrated language when the evidence is incomplete: “may,” “likely,” “suggests,” “does not say.”
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs to brand abstractions.
- Put the takeaway first. Keep one idea per sentence.
- Use the candidate's own facts. Label examples and placeholders clearly.
- Show the original beside any suggested rewrite.
- Use the same term for the same thing across the product.
- Make actions literal: “See my first read,” “Read a sample report,” “Delete report.”
- Use lightness only when it makes the message easier to receive.
- Let the product celebrate a clear result: “There it is: a result with real scale.”
- Aim playful language at the document, the process, or bad advice—never the candidate.

## Use sparingly

These words are useful when they are precise, but become machine-like when repeated:

- signal
- proof
- impact
- first read
- lands
- clarity
- recruiter-ready

Prefer the concrete meaning: role, result, scope, decision, question, detail, review, or evidence.

## Avoid

- Hiring promises: “land more interviews,” “get hired faster,” “beat the ATS.”
- Fake certainty: “This is what recruiters think,” “This will get you hired.”
- Fake intimacy: “Trust me,” “I've got you,” “We know exactly how you feel.”
- Generic AI language: unlock, elevate, transform, seamless, empower, journey, game-changing.
- Formulaic slogans, repeated tricolons, and repeated “not X, but Y” constructions.
- Cheerful error messages, joke density, or praise that is not supported by the resume.
- Snark, roasts, hustle language, meme copy, or a mascot voice pasted onto serious moments.
- Suggestions that silently add facts, metrics, tools, or ownership.

## Tone by moment

| Moment | Tone | What the user needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marketing | Buoyant, quotable, lightly playful | A recognizable hiring truth and the feeling that improvement is possible |
| Upload | Calm and explicit | What to add, what is optional, what happens next |
| Analysis | Transparent | What is being reviewed and how long it may take |
| Report | Precise, generous, and calibrated | What is working, one open question, and what to change |
| Rewrite | Protective of facts | The original, the suggestion, and any missing detail |
| Research | Clear, specific, and intellectually honest | The finding, evidence type, source quality, practical meaning, and limit |
| Pricing | Blunt and literal | Exact cost, renewal terms, cancellation, and what is included |
| Error | Accountable and useful | What happened and the next available action |

## Resource-guide voice

Resource guides should feel like calm backstage knowledge, not generic career coaching.

- Teach the company-side mechanism the reader needs to understand: the range, level, approval chain, written term, or missing fact.
- Give the reader a sentence they can actually say, followed by what that sentence makes clear. Do not invent “what the recruiter hears.”
- Use a specific, supportable claim beside any memorable line. Charm may open the door; the useful fact has to walk through it.
- Avoid negotiation theater: no chess metaphors as strategy, bluffing, “winning,” “losing leverage,” or pretending every employer follows the same rules.
- Never turn a common practice into a universal rule. Salary law, equity, taxes, deadlines, bands, and benefits need a source, date, jurisdiction, or explicit limit.
- Make related guides do different jobs. The general offer guide builds the counter. The tech guide makes a complex package legible.

The most ownable resource pattern is **request → basis → decision**. A good counter must survive a handoff from the recruiter to the manager or compensation team.

## Score language

Say what the score does before stating its limits. The score summarizes four parts of the resume review and helps the user decide where to start. It does not estimate interview or offer probability. Lead with the written finding and keep the number visually and verbally secondary. Always explain what drove it and what the user can do next.

## Signature lines

Use a signature line only in marketing, and only when it states a useful product truth. Do not use signature lines on research, methodology, privacy, pricing, or report findings.

- Good work deserves a good read.
- Let's make the good work easy to see.
- Do not add a number because a tool asked. Add the detail you can stand behind.

## Research language

Research exists to make the advice more trustworthy, not to make the brand sound original.

Every research page should make five things easy to find:

1. What question the page answers.
2. Whether the basis is published research, documented recruiter practice, or RIYP interpretation.
3. What the source supports.
4. What the user should do with that information.
5. What the evidence does not establish.

Use normal labels such as “What the research found,” “What this means for your resume,” “Sources,” and “Limitations.” Avoid lines that sound coined, self-congratulatory, or cute about evidence.

## Tone boundaries

Marketing gets the highest voltage. Reports do not imitate it.

- Marketing can use contrast, rhythm, and one small, surprising turn of phrase.
- Research should answer the question, show its sources and limits, and explain the practical implication.
- Reports stay evidence-led and calibrated because the user is making decisions from them.
- Errors, privacy, and billing never use wit.
- Never turn the candidate, recruiter, or job search into the butt of the joke.
- Keep humor out of trust surfaces. A warm, natural sentence is enough.

## Review checklist

Before shipping copy, ask:

1. Could a competing resume product use this sentence unchanged?
2. Does every judgment point back to visible evidence?
3. Are we more certain than the source allows?
4. Are we criticizing the resume rather than the person?
5. Does the user know what happens next?
6. Did we preserve the candidate's facts and control?
